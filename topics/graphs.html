Graphs

Eleven pages on graphs: how to store them, how to walk them (BFS, DFS), how to find shortest paths (Dijkstra, Bellman-Ford, Floyd-Warshall), how to connect everything cheaply (Kruskal, Prim), and how to order and check them (topological sort, cycle detection, Union-Find).

Why it matters: roads, networks, dependencies and social links are all graphs. Knowing which algorithm fits which question, and its precondition, is most of the work.

    Key points

    • Adjacency lists make traversals $O(V + E)$ because they store only real edges.
    • BFS gives shortest unweighted paths because it reaches vertices layer by layer.
    • Dijkstra needs non-negative weights because it declares distances final as soon as they are the smallest.
    • Bellman-Ford and Floyd-Warshall allow negative edges because they never finalise a distance early.
    • Kruskal and Prim are both greedy because the cheapest edge across any cut is always safe for a minimum spanning tree.

    Video talking points

    Use these 5 bullets as your script outline. Say each one in your own words. Do not read them: glance at them and speak.

    1. Hook (10 sec). Ask: what do GPS routes, social networks and build systems have in common?
    2. The big picture (30 sec). They are graphs: vertices joined by edges. Most graph algorithms are a traversal, BFS or DFS, plus a rule about which vertex to handle next.
    3. Weights change everything (30 sec). Without weights, BFS gives shortest paths. With non-negative weights use Dijkstra; with negative weights Bellman-Ford; for all pairs, Floyd-Warshall.
    4. How to choose (45 sec). Walk the "Which one to pick?" flowchart: shortest paths by weights and sources; cheapest network, Kruskal or Prim; task order, topological sort; loops, cycle detection; "same group?", Union-Find.
    5. One takeaway (10 sec). Name the question first, then the algorithm follows: distance, connection, order or cycle.
