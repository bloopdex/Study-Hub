Linear structures

Eight ways to keep items in a line. Arrays give instant access by index; linked lists make relinking cheap; stacks, queues and deques restrict where you add and remove; a priority queue always serves the most urgent item.

Why it matters: the structure you pick decides which operations are $O(1)$ and which are $O(n)$. Choosing well is often a bigger speed-up than any clever algorithm.

    The eight structures

    Array

    Contiguous slots, found by address arithmetic.

    O(1) access · O(n) insert in the middle

    Singly linked list

    Nodes chained by next pointers.

    O(1) push front · O(n) search

    Doubly linked list

    Next and prev pointers in every node.

    O(1) delete a known node · 2 pointers per node

    Circular linked list

    The last node points back to the first.

    Which one to pick?

    flowchart TD

  A -->|"the oldest"| Q["Queue"]
  A -->|"newest or oldest, both ends"| D["Deque"]
  A -->|"the most urgent"| P["Priority queue"]
  A -->|"any item"| B{"Need access by index?"}
  B -->|yes| AR["Array"]
  B -->|no| C{"Delete nodes you already hold, or walk backwards?"}
  C -->|yes| DL["Doubly linked list"]
  C -->|no| E{"Cycle through items forever?"}
  E -->|yes| CL["Circular linked list"]
  E -->|no| SL["Singly linked list"]

    Key points

    • Arrays give $O(1)$ access because the address of item $i$ is computed directly.
    • Linked lists give $O(1)$ relinking because inserting next to a known node changes only pointers, not positions.
    • Stacks, queues and deques are $O(1)$ because they only ever touch the ends.
    • A priority queue costs $O(\log n)$ because a heap keeps only partial order, along one root-to-leaf path.
    • Pick by your most frequent operation because every structure makes some operations cheap by making others expensive.

    Video talking points

    Use these 5 bullets as your script outline. Say each one in your own words. Do not read them: glance at them and speak.

    1. Hook (10 sec). Ask: why do programmers argue about arrays versus linked lists when both just hold items in a row?
    2. The big picture (30 sec). Arrays give $O(1)$ access by index but $O(n)$ inserts in the middle. Linked lists give $O(1)$ inserts at a known node but $O(n)$ access. Stacks, queues, deques and priority queues are rules for which end you may touch.
    3. Access rules (30 sec). A stack is last in, first out; a queue is first in, first out; a deque allows both ends; a priority queue serves the most urgent item, with $O(\log n)$ operations on a heap.
    4. How to choose (45 sec). Walk the "Which one to pick?" flowchart: random access, array; frequent inserts next to known nodes, linked list; undo or brackets, stack; fair waiting line, queue; both ends, deque; most urgent first, priority queue.
    5. One takeaway (10 sec). Choose the structure by the operations you do most often, because each one makes some operations cheap and others expensive.
