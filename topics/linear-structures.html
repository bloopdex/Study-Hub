Linear structures

Eight ways to keep items in a line. Arrays give instant access by index; linked lists make relinking cheap; stacks, queues and deques restrict where you add and remove; a priority queue always serves the most urgent item.

Why it matters: the structure you pick decides which operations are $O(1)$ and which are $O(n)$. Choosing well is often a bigger speed-up than any clever algorithm.

    The eight structures

    Singly linked list

    Nodes chained by next pointers.

    Next and prev pointers in every node.

    Which one to pick?

    flowchart TD
  A{"Which item do you remove next?"} -->|"the newest"| S["Stack"]
  A -->|"the oldest"| Q["Queue"]
  A -->|"newest or oldest, both ends"| D["Deque"]
  A -->|"the most urgent"| P["Priority queue"]
  A -->|"any item"| B{"Need access by index?"}
  B -->|yes| AR["Array"]
  B -->|no| C{"Delete nodes you already hold, or walk backwards?"}
  C -->|yes| DL["Doubly linked list"]
  C -->|no| E{"Cycle through items forever?"}
  E -->|yes| CL["Circular linked list"]
  E -->|no| SL["Singly linked list"]

    Key points

    • Arrays give $O(1)$ access because the address of item $i$ is computed directly.
    • Linked lists give $O(1)$ relinking because inserting next to a known node changes only pointers, not positions.
    • Stacks, queues and deques are $O(1)$ because they only ever touch the ends.
    • A priority queue costs $O(\log n)$ because a heap keeps only partial order, along one root-to-leaf path.
    • Pick by your most frequent operation because every structure makes some operations cheap by making others expensive.
