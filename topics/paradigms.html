Algorithm paradigms

Five ways of thinking about a problem rather than five algorithms: split it (divide and conquer), grab the best option (greedy), remember sub-answers (dynamic programming), try and undo (backtracking), and recognise when no fast method is known (NP-completeness).

Why it matters: most new problems are solved by recognising which paradigm fits. The paradigm tells you the shape of the algorithm and roughly what it will cost.

    Which one to pick?

    flowchart TD
  A{"Does the problem split into smaller copies?"} -->|"yes, independent parts"| DC["Divide and conquer"]
  A -->|"yes, but parts repeat"| DP["Dynamic programming"]
  A -->|no| B{"Is there a provably safe 'best next choice'?"}
  B -->|yes| G["Greedy"]
  B -->|no| C{"Is it a known NP-complete problem?"}
  C -->|yes| NP["Use approximation, heuristics, or exact search on small inputs"]
  C -->|no| BT["Backtracking with pruning"]

    Key points

    • Divide and conquer works because independent subproblems can be solved separately and combined.
    • Dynamic programming beats plain recursion because it never solves the same subproblem twice.
    • Greedy is only correct with a proof because a locally best choice can block a better overall answer.
    • Backtracking is practical because pruning removes whole branches of the search early.
    • NP-completeness changes the goal because no polynomial exact algorithm is known for those problems.

    Video talking points

    Use these 5 bullets as your script outline. Say each one in your own words. Do not read them: glance at them and speak.

    1. Hook (10 sec). Ask: when you face a brand-new problem, how do you even start designing an algorithm?
    2. The big picture (30 sec). Most algorithms follow a few patterns: split into independent parts (divide and conquer), commit to the best choice now (greedy), reuse overlapping answers (dynamic programming), or try and undo (backtracking).
    3. Greedy vs DP (30 sec). Greedy is fast but only correct when its choice is provably safe; DP compares every choice and is always correct when subproblems overlap, at the price of a table.
    4. How to choose (45 sec). Walk the "Which one to pick?" flowchart: independent subproblems, divide and conquer; a safe local choice, greedy; overlapping subproblems, DP; constraints to satisfy, backtracking; and if the problem is NP-complete, expect heuristics.
    5. One takeaway (10 sec). Recognise the structure of the problem and the paradigm almost picks itself.
